# Definitions engine

Status: current code guide for `lib/definitions/`, checked in November 2026. It is used by [Brunch orders](../processes/brunch-orders/PLAN.md). The earlier Brunch weekend engine it grew out of is [archived](../archive/BRUNCH-WEEKEND-ENGINE-GUIDE.md).

## Contract

A process supplies a `DefinitionProcess`: its ID and a `DefinitionSubject` with the records, lookups, typed columns, proposal fields and reader words. Brunch orders declares this in `lib/processes/brunch-orders/process.ts`; the engine does not hard-code products or orders. Nor does it hard-code a sheet: `DataShape.source` names the kind of source the data is read from (absent, `google-sheets`), and `sourceOf` in `records.ts` gives that kind's words and addressing from `lib/sources/model.ts` (`SOURCE_KINDS`, and the addressing beside it). Every read problem, proposal problem, prompt and schema field that names a resource says the kind's word for it (a sheet's tab, a database's table) and the source's own noun; every cited value, cell change and written block is addressed as the kind addresses it: a sheet's `Stock!B3` and `B2:C12`, a database's `usage[D-05].bikes_present` (a row by its key where one names it alone, else `usage[row 5].dock_ref`, by the number the data shows) and `rows 1-2, columns 1-3`, which a write reads back from. The names `KeptRead.tabs`, `DataShape.records.tabs`, `lookups[].tab`, `output.tab`, `Application.tab`, `CellChange.cell` and the `no-tab` answer problem are historical: stored or declared contracts, each meaning a resource whatever the kind calls it. The current read joins Products, Stock, Sales and Deliveries by product key and looks up Parameters by category. A process may also declare timing (`DataShape.timing`): a time zone, the weekly opening of the planned period where it has one (`opens`, optional), moments, and columns of hours for formulas (`since`, `until`), all worked out from when the data was read, never the wall clock. A moment is a weekday and time-of-day column pair placed by its kind (observed: the last time it came round by the read; deadline: the last before the period opens; next: the first after the read), or one `date` or `datetime` column (`{ at, label? }`), the instant its cell holds: a date with no time is the start of that day in the time zone its type declares, as `interpretCell` reads it. Without `opens` there is no `start` moment, and a weekly deadline or next moment, or hours `until` a weekday and time, is a declaration error, reported with the read's problems where the declaration is first read; so is a dated moment on a column not typed as a date. Each moment is kept on the record as its hours from the read, blank where its cells are.

One definition has a name, the exact instructions text it interprets, a `check` or `number` result, optional check consequence, quoted constants and groups, a formula, and examples. A formula may read the current record, proposal fields and earlier definitions. `lib/definitions/types.ts` validates the saved set.

A number's formula is written by the model. A check's is not: the model fills in one of five fixed shapes (`shape-schema.ts`), and code writes both the formula and the sentence a person approves from it (`shapes.ts`), so the two cannot say different things. The shapes are limit, step, only when, target and flag (flag only for a check a person decides), each optionally limited to a group or to conditions. What a blank cell means is not the model's to say: it is a fact about the data, kept on the data profile (below); a shape stored before that keeps its own `if_blank` and its formula exactly. Conditions are joined by "and" only. A target's margin is a percentage: a number such as 10 is divided by 100, and a column typed as a percentage, which holds its fraction already, is not. Names in a shape are short references (`column:<column>`, `value:<id>`, `constant:<id>`, `proposed:<field>`, `moment:<moment>`, `hours_since:<moment>`); a bare name is read where only one thing could be meant, and so is a name under the wrong kind (`column:<earlier value>`), a field written `proposed:<field>`, a missing field where the process has only one, and a group of `null`. For a check that stops a proposal, code lets a proposal of nothing through wherever the process declares what nothing is (a proposal field's `none`). Time is said only with moments (`DataShape.timing.moments`), each a weekday and time column pair that is observed, a deadline or the next occurrence, compared before or after one another, the period's start or the read; `hours_since` reads only an observed weekly moment or a dated one. Whether a dated moment has happened by the read is known per record, not per declaration, so a rule reading hours since one fails on a record whose date is after the read, and its sentence says so: the hours are never negative, so a sign cannot turn "more than" into "less than" unseen. Refusing `hours_since` on dated moments would have left "not inspected for N days" unsayable. The worked example shows a dated moment as its date, with its time for a date-time column, in the process's time zone. A shape never sees the signed hours worked out for formulas, so it has no sign to get wrong. A rule no shape can say is refused, naming why (it needs "or", other arithmetic, or data that is not there), and a person rephrases it. Checks accepted before shapes keep their formulas and still run.

## Data profile

A read's column types used to be inferred afresh from each read, so one decimal stock count or one column blank throughout made an accepted rulebook fail to type-check. A data profile (`profile.ts`) fixes them. `profileOf` proposes one from a read: one entry per column a formula can reach, named as a shape names it (`<column>`, `<lookup>.<column>`), typed as the process declares (`DataShape.types`) or else from every non-blank cell (whole numbers, decimals, real checkboxes as yes/no, otherwise text; null for a column blank throughout), with a label, whether blanks were seen, and what a blank means for a rule (`blank`: skip, fail, or null until answered). A person confirms it; every change (`setColumnType`, `setColumnLabel`, `setColumnBlank`) gives a new id and clears the confirmation. Profiles are kept in `fixtures/<process>/profile/`: `current.json` under review, `<profile id>.json` for each confirmed one, written once (`saveProfile`, `putProfile`, `loadProfile`, `loadConfirmedProfile`). A set records the confirmed profile it was drafted on (`profileId`), and the rulebook fingerprint includes it; a set without one reads as before. `withProfile` gives the process with its data held to a profile, so every `owner.subject.data` reads it; `ownerFor` loads the one a set names, `profiledFor` refuses a set whose profile was not loaded, and a draft starts on the kept profile only once confirmed (`ownerToDraft`). Against a profile, `records.ts` keeps each column's type whatever the read holds: a cell that does not fit is a read problem by its cell, a whole number in a decimal column is a decimal, a column blank throughout stays typed, a column the profile lists that the read lacks is a problem, and so is a declared type that disagrees with the profile; a column the profile does not list is typed from the read.

A check's shape no longer says what a blank cell means. Each column it reads takes the profile's answer: skip columns form the leading guard (the rule does not apply, and Propose says so), fail columns and unanswered ones must be present for the rule to hold, and with no profile every column fails. A moment is skipped only where both its columns say skip; the start and the read are never blank. The sentence says each meaning where the read has such a blank. Accept refuses a rule reading a column with blanks whose meaning is unanswered (`unansweredBlanks`), naming it. Once the profile is changed and confirmed again, `rebaseSet` moves the rulebook under review onto it: it writes each such check's formula again from its shape, versions included, reviews every definition again on the read, compares its versions again, and withdraws acceptance only from what changed or no longer passes and what reads it. Revisions are never touched.

A person follows this in the Rules step, through the rules route (`app/api/dev/brunch-orders/rules/route.ts`) and its panel (`components/brunch-orders/data-profile.tsx`). With no profile kept, the panel says how many columns the run's read has and how many have blank cells, and Review the columns keeps what `profileOf` suggests, under review (`profile`). Columns whose blanks have no meaning yet come first, each asking what a blank means ("The rule doesn't apply" or "The rule fails"); the rest are one press away. A column's words can be changed, and its type where the app inferred it; a declared type says the process sets it. Any change puts the profile back under review (`profileColumn`). Confirm the data (`confirmProfile`) confirms it and moves the rulebook under review, if one is not yet accepted whole, onto it with `rebaseSet`, on the read it was reviewed on, so an answer takes effect without a redraft. The route refuses a draft until the kept profile is confirmed, though the engine itself still drafts without one. Once the rules are drafted the panel folds to one line, and a card whose Accept was refused for an unanswered blank leads back to it.

## From instructions to acceptance

1. The development read route loads a captured or live five-tab read and keeps it under an ID. `records.ts` reports missing tabs, bad keys and invalid typed cells.
2. `find.ts` identifies definitions from one instructions version. It accounts for every sentence as a quantity's working, a check or only context, and code refuses an answer that leaves one out, points at a definition it did not list, or lists one no sentence states; one more try is allowed, then the draft stops and nothing is replaced. The account is kept on the set (`accounting`), and the review shows what was read as context. `draft.ts` starts a server-side draft and records progress; `write.ts` writes each definition in order.
3. `formulas.ts` type-checks and evaluates CEL. `checks.ts` tests the definition against its read, quoted constants and groups, dependencies and worked examples. A formula must read the data and everything it declares, and a check's examples must show it holding and failing wherever the read lets it do both. Whether the read lets it is found by trying the formula on every record at a few fixed values and at values taken from the record itself (below), so a check that fails only at a record's own limit is not taken for one that cannot fail. The model's check tool uses these checks.
4. The person confirms the intent and accepts or denies each definition. `decide.ts` reruns the full review against the read before acceptance. A denial can carry a note for the next write.
5. `store.ts` reads saved sets through the schema and writes a temporary file before renaming it over the old set. Each instructions version has a folder, `fixtures/<process>/rulebooks/<version>-<instructions-hash>/`, found by the hash: `current.json` is the rulebook under review, and `<rulebook id>.json` is a revision: an accepted rulebook, written once and never again. The id is a fingerprint of what was accepted (`fingerprintOf`). When the decision that completes a rulebook is made, `decide.ts` also keeps on it `acceptedKeys`: the keys of the records of the read it was reviewed on, read through the data profile it names, so a record the data gains later can be told from them (an optional field: a rulebook accepted before has none and behaves as before, and its id is unchanged, since the fingerprint includes the keys only where there are some). `revisions.json` records each revision's parent, reason (`accepted` when the decision that completes the rulebook is made, `migrated` when a script rewrites it, `used` when a run first uses one never frozen), time and stored-shape version, and the head. Revisions are reached only through `putRevision`, `loadRevision`, `listRevisions` and `headOf`, so a database can replace the files without the engine changing.
6. `calculate.ts` works every accepted calculation out for every record, and has the model cross-check them; cross-checks are kept in `data/<process>/cross-checks/`.
7. `propose.ts` has the model propose values for every record, trying them against the accepted checks (`try_proposals`) before it submits. Code checks every proposed value again (`checkProposal`), verifies every cited cell, and keeps its own verdicts: ready, decide, blocked, or unchecked where the rulebook has no check to run. A record the model leaves is checked with each field at its declared `none`, and a field that declares none is not read as anything: a check reading it does not apply; a calculation that cannot be worked out on a record fails every check that needs it on that record, by name, and leaves the rest running; a value below a field's declared `min` blocks. Proposals are kept in `data/<process>/proposals/`; a person's decisions on them belong to the run. A rulebook with `acceptedKeys` and an accepted definition holding a group with records in it gives a record outside those keys one more verdict of its own, `new:record`, which a person decides: the record is new since the rules were accepted and they name groups by their words (shown), so which of them it belongs to is not for the formula to guess, as "in the group, or else" would. A rulebook with no groups, or no keys, adds nothing. A check written "not in scope, or the rule" (its outermost `||` led by a negation that reads no proposal field) does not apply to a record out of scope: it holds, its working stops at the guard, and Propose says "Doesn't apply".
8. `fits.ts` type-checks a revision against the read it is used on, as Accept does, without the examples. A revision is kept as it was accepted, so one written before a column's type changed is refused on use, naming each definition that no longer fits, and the run is offered the current rules when they fit.

9. `apply.ts` writes a decided proposal to the output the process declares, through an `OutputSheet` it is given. It reads the sheet again, refuses a write if a data tab has changed, runs every check again at that moment, reads the block back, and keeps the record in `data/<process>/applied/`. A record left as it is is checked on the sheet as read now, though nothing is written for it. Undo restores the block only while it holds what was written.

`app/api/dev/brunch-orders/rules/route.ts` exposes get, draft, intent, write and decide actions for this development flow; `calculate/route.ts`, `propose/route.ts` and `apply/route.ts` beside it do the same for those steps. None is a public process-definition API. The [development log](../processes/brunch-orders/DEVELOPMENT-LOG.md) records why its checks and approval boundary changed.

`debug.ts` says what code finds of a draft without asking any model, for the debug drawers on the Rules step (`components/brunch-orders/rules-debug.tsx`): each thing `reviewBreakdown` holds a definition to, run again and shown together, and what its formula gives on every record at a value a person tries. The values offered are the proposal's own fields the formula reads, with the process's words for them, so the drawers know no process. They also list the columns each rule reads, itself and through the calculations it reads, and those no rule reads, counting the columns a worked-out column or a moment comes from as read. A wrong reading can pass every check and agree with all the data when a column happens to be 0, so this is for a person to compare: rules about one thing that read different columns. `review` is built from the same parts, so the two cannot disagree. What the model did on each attempt is not recorded yet.

A draft writes each definition twice from the same input (`writeAgreed`), neither write seeing the other. Two checks with the same shape agree; otherwise `differencesBetween` compares how the two behave on every record at a range of values: a fixed list (every whole number to 20, then steps of 5, and a few large ones) and, for each record, whole numbers taken from its own numbers (`ownValues`): what its row, its lookups' rows, the definitions before it and the versions' constants give, each with one either side, and a few whole multiples of the small ones so a step is met, not below the proposal field's declared `min`, at most 200 a record, those the formulas name first. `reachOf` tries the same values, so "it cannot fail on this read" is not concluded from 0, 1 and 1,000,000 alone. Where the versions read more than one proposal field, each is also tried alone with the others at 0, since with every field at one value the same rule on two fields cannot be told apart. Two versions are still compared only at values tried, so none found is not proof: two versions that differ only for a value no number of the record leads to are reported as agreeing. If they differ, the definition keeps working for what reads it but cannot be accepted until a person chooses a version (`choose`, which takes the version's shape with its formula), shown as the sentence each would check. Accepting a definition with a shape refuses one whose formula is not the one its shape gives. A third write is only for a write that failed the checks or refused: a refusal is never compared, any shape wins over it, and a rule is refused only when every write refuses. After two refusals, a draft with no model chosen writes once more on the drafting model (Flash): its shape is compared with one written before if there is one, and otherwise stands alone, unconfirmed; its refusal stands. A model a person chose is kept throughout. A stored shape keeps only the fields its kind reads, in one spelling (bare field, no group where none was meant, every reference as `kind:name`; `canonicalShape`), and a write keeps only the constants and group the shape names: `check_definition` drops the rest and says so, rather than sending the model back to fix what the shape never reads. Conditions written in `when` on a kind that has none of its own are read as its scope, or refused where a scope is given as well, never dropped: dropped, a rule for some records became a rule for all of them. Where versions are split, "Which is right?" asks Flash once, or Flash-Lite where Flash is busy or spent (`advise.ts`), which version the instructions support. The answer quotes the instructions, and code checks the quote is really there. It opens in the assistant as advice and is never kept or applied: a third reading of the same words is a vote, and the vote was wrong on 2 of 6 rules, so the person still chooses on the card. The development log has the numbers behind this.

`limits.ts` keeps requests within what Google allows: each request of a tool loop waits for a slot, a per-minute refusal is waited out in place, and a busy model is tried once more, so a loop keeps its progress. Limits are learned from refusals and kept in `data/model-limits.json` for a week; `GEMINI_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE` overrides them. What cannot be waited out reaches `calls.ts`, which moves to the next model of the family, or stops if all are spent. `app/api/dev/model-limits` reports how a model's quota stands.

## Conformance

The engine is held to a second process that exists only in tests: bike-share docks planned each week (`lib/definitions/conformance.fixture.ts`, run by `conformance.test.ts`). It shares nothing with Brunch orders and differs from it on purpose: three record tabs keyed by `dock_ref`, two lookups whose `via`, `key` and `as` all differ (one reached through the third record tab), two proposal fields (bikes, with `none` and `min`; a fare, with neither), decimals with whole numbers among them, percentages as cells and as text, money, yes/no checkboxes, a date column, a text column with blanks and a numeric column blank throughout, moments under other names in another time zone, and the same process with no timing at all, and read from a database rather than a sheet (`TABLE_DOCKS`). Variants of its read carry a missing lookup row, a key listed twice, a decimal in a whole-number column, a blank a calculation needs and a record new since acceptance. Every expectation (read problems, profile, formulas, sentences, calculations and each verdict) is worked out by hand, and the test runs the whole flow on it: read, profile, find, write twice with stand-in models (agreeing, splitting, refusing), accept through to a frozen revision, rebase onto a newer profile, calculate, check, propose and write to a sheet held in memory, and undo. On the database it asserts, word for word, the read problems (a missing table, a key listed twice, a cell that does not fit the profile, a profiled column the read lacks), the citation field the model is given (`table`), the citations kept and each problem a bad one gives, and a write, read back and undo on a table held in memory. `leaks.test.ts` gathers every word Brunch orders supplies (tabs, headers, record words, fields, lookups, moments, keys and names) from its declaration and captured read, and fails if an engine source holds one in code, strings or comments, outside a short list of phrases where the word is the engine's own; it also fails if any string or template in an engine source says tab, sheet or spreadsheet or holds an A1 address, outside a short list of historical identifiers and module paths. Dated moments are held to it on its `last_inspected` column (flag and only-when rules, a date-time variant, a blank and a future date, a process with no weekly opening, a misdeclared one), and `weekly-timing.test.ts` holds Brunch's weekly moments to the hours every record had at three read times before dated moments existed.

## Present limit

Writing has been tested only against a sheet and a table held in memory, and the Brunch orders plan marks history as a later phase. A stored accepted set and a captured read are not a durable production review record. What the engine cannot express, each recorded in `conformance.test.ts` under "limits":

- A date column is a moment only where the process declares it one; a duration in days or weeks cannot be compared with hours since a moment, as a constant must be quoted in the instructions' own unit.
- A source of any kind is held as resources of rows under a header row, and a write is one rectangular block of one resource; the database kind exists only in tests, and screens outside the engine (`lib/process/model-run.ts`, the run and output components) still word citations and cells as a sheet's.
- A record is one row of each record tab; a key listed twice is a read problem.
- A check sees one record at a time.
- Proposal fields are whole numbers.
- A constant is checked against the words that state it, so an amount written in a currency ("S$3.00") states 3, while a money column holds minor units (300).
- Instructions are read as English: numbers in words, weekday names and plurals in group words.
- Types are declared by column header (`DataShape.types`), so a record column and a lookup column of the same name share one declared type.
